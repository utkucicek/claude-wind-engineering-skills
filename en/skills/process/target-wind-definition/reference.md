# Reference: CWE prestandard Chapter 3 — clause table

Source: ASCE "Prestandard for Use of Computational Wind Engineering in Building Design", Chapter 3. The summaries are written in our own sentences. "PDF page" is the PDF page order (printed page = PDF − 18).

Type: **MANDATORY** = "shall" in the text; **RECOMMENDED** = "should"; **COMMENTARY** = "Commentary" paragraph; **INFORMATIVE** = explanatory main text.

Chapter 3 contains 4 POs and 6 ACs: PO 3-1, 3-2, 3-3, 3-4; AC 3-1a, 3-1b, 3-1c, 3-2, 3-3, 3-4.

## §3.2 Assessment of the local wind climate

| Clause | Type | Summary | Numerical criterion | PDF page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PO 3-1 (§3.2) | MANDATORY | When wind-induced load effects and responses are assessed by CWE, the aerodynamic data from the simulation are combined with a site-specific wind climate model. | — | 33 |
| §3.2 commentary | COMMENTARY | The basic speed return periods for Risk Category I, II, III, IV are 300, 700, 1,700 and 3,000 years respectively. ASCE/SEI 7-22 gives non-directional basic speeds that include extratropical storms, hurricanes and thunderstorms, and separate tornado speeds that depend on the effective plan area. As an alternative, in compliance with ASCE/SEI 7-22 §26.5.3, the basic speeds can be estimated from long-term local/regional full-scale records, Monte Carlo synthetic records, reanalysis or WRF modeling. | MRI: 300 / 700 / 1,700 / 3,000 years | 33 |
| §3.2.1 | MANDATORY | If long-term full-scale records are used for the probabilistic climate model, the records are taken from reliable stations (e.g. conforming to World Meteorological Organization criteria). | — | 33 |
| AC 3-1a (§3.2.1) | MANDATORY | Multidecadal records are used; a shorter period is accepted only if it is justified and documented. | "Multidecadal" (no exact number of years given) | 33 |
| §3.2.1 | MANDATORY | The different storm types in the long-term records are separated; where relevant the records are corrected for exposure before the statistical analysis. | — | 33 |
| AC 3-1b (§3.2.1) | MANDATORY | In hurricane-prone regions the use of speeds recorded at local stations alone is not permitted; Monte Carlo simulation is done with basin-scale hurricane track records. | — | 33–34 |
| AC 3-1c (§3.2.1) | MANDATORY | If reanalysis data sets (from a global model only, or downscaled with a regional model) are used to characterize site conditions, their performance is cross-validated against meteorological observations, with particular attention to extreme events. | — | 34 |
| §3.2.1 commentary | COMMENTARY/RECOMMENDED | Examining more than one station is recommended. Before the analysis the data quality, the measuring equipment and changes of location/type over time should be reviewed; obstructions near the anemometer (e.g. a hangar) can distort the records. By characterizing the z0 upstream of the station, the extratropical storm records should be transferred to uniform open terrain (e.g. ASCE/SEI 7-22 Exposure C); the change of roughness over time (e.g. urbanization) should be considered. Records from stations affected by topography should be corrected (§3.3.2). In a hurricane a ground station represents a single realization and can lose data through damage/failure; satellite, dropsonde, Doppler radar, buoy network and aircraft reconnaissance provide far more comprehensive sampling, and simulation techniques should be validated against them. | — | 34 |
| §3.2.2 (1) | MANDATORY | The climate model is the probabilistic combination of the relevant storm types for the study area. | — | 34 |
| §3.2.2 (2) | MANDATORY | The climate model covers the whole range of return periods relevant to the risk category of the building(s) studied. | — | 34 |
| §3.2.2 (3) | MANDATORY | The climate model accounts for the effect of the directionality of extreme wind events. | — | 34 |
| §3.2.2 | MANDATORY | The climate model accounts for the exposure(s) of the project site before it is combined with the simulation output to assess design loads and structural response (§3.3). | — | 34 |
| §3.2.2 commentary | COMMENTARY/RECOMMENDED | A joint speed-direction probability distribution can be built from measured or synthetic long-term records; an approximate representation with a two- or five-parameter Weibull is common; the directional Weibull parameters are combined with the aerodynamic data by, e.g., the up-crossing method. Great care should be taken with the fit of the tail of the distribution. If the extreme speed distribution is needed, an appropriate extreme value analysis should be used; the method of independent storms is regarded as one of the most robust approaches. In a mixed climate the extreme distribution of each storm type should be assessed separately and then combined. Special assessment should be made where topography affects the local regime. | — | 34–35 |

| §3.3.3 commentary – downburst | COMMENTARY | In the outflow region of a downburst the speed increases only up to a few hundred feet above the ground and decreases above that (Figure 3-3). Models mentioned: Oseguera and Bowles (1988), Vicroy (1991) and the Oseguera-Bowles-Vicroy model; Wood et al. (2001), an empirical model for the mean radial velocity profile of a downburst that is stationary in space (Equation 3-19; Umax is the largest horizontal mean speed in the profile, δ the height at which the speed falls to half of Umax). Xhelaj et al. (2020): the Wood model is more suitable when the largest radial speed is close to the ground, the Oseguera-Bowles-Vicroy model when it is higher up. Holmes and Oliver (2000): vector sum of the stationary radial speed and the translational motion of the storm cell. The relationship between the translating cell and the boundary layer wind in which it sits, and the turbulence properties of these events, are not yet fully known. | The coefficients of Equation 3-19 cannot be read reliably in the source text | 40–41 |
| §3.3.3 commentary – tornado | COMMENTARY | Tornadoes can have a single-cell (moderate swirl ratios) or two-cell (much larger swirl ratios) vortex structure. As the target, the vertical profiles of the three velocity components at various radial distances are needed; the largest mean tangential speed, its radial position and its height above ground are the basic target parameters. Qualified full-scale measurements are few; in radar measurements the part closest to the ground is generally missing. Models mentioned: Fujita (1978), Xu and Hangan (2009) (empirical); Baker and Sterling (2017) (equation-based). The simplest model is the Rankine vortex (Equation 3-20): solid-body rotation inside (speed proportional to radius), a region decaying by a power law outside. | Part missing in radar data: the first ~100 ft (~30 m) above ground; decay exponent α typically between −0.5 and −0.7 | 41 |

## Unclear points / gaps in the source

- Figure 3-1 (tornado-prone region), Figure 3-2 (topography methods) and Figure 3-3 (downburst structure) are present in the plain-text source only as captions.
- The term "Step 0" in the §3.3.2 commentary is not defined in the main text of Chapters 3–4; it appears to be defined in Figure 3-2 (preliminary topography simulation), but the content of the figure could not be verified.
- The order of AC 3-2 and AC 3-3 in the text appears reversed (first PO 3-3 / AC 3-3, then AC 3-2); the AC 3-2 bracket is at the head of the five-parameter list and belongs to PO 3-2.
- Chapter 3 gives no numerical tolerance for matching the targets.
